Order the neurons of a connectome however you like and some synaptic weight still points backward. The minimum over all orderings is a property of the wiring, not of any algorithm — the share of connectivity that no feedforward hierarchy can account for. We measure it with certificates on both sides, a cycle packing below and an exhibited ordering above, in four connectomes spanning two phyla. In Caenorhabditis elegans the two bounds meet exactly, at 3.81%. We first asked whether this quantity grows with nervous-system size, and report that it does not: degree-matched random graphs reproduce the entire pattern, including its non-monotone step, from network density alone. What survives that control is a different result, pointing the opposite way. Compared against random graphs carrying its own degree sequence under the same solver effort, **every nervous system we measured is less irreducibly recurrent than chance**, by ratios of 0.64, 0.67, 0.79 and 0.90. Zhao and Zhou reported this direction for the 297-node C. elegans network in 2016 [16], against a weaker null; we confirm it with a degree-preserving null, by weight rather than arc count, with certificates, and on connectomes orders of magnitude larger. This holds although the real connectomes carry three to fifty-five times more weight on reciprocated connections than their nulls do — the property that most directly forces backward weight. Real nervous systems are therefore arranged so that their recurrence is unusually resolvable into a hierarchy, not unusually abundant.
