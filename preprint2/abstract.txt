Order the neurons of a connectome however you like and some synaptic weight will still point backward. The minimum of that quantity over all possible orderings is a property of the wiring rather than of any algorithm: the fraction of a nervous system's connectivity that no feedforward hierarchy can account for. We call it irreducible recurrence, and we measure it with certificates on both sides — a cycle packing that lower-bounds it and an explicit ordering that upper-bounds it — in four connectomes spanning two phyla and a 460-fold range in size. In Caenorhabditis elegans the two bounds meet: the packing forces exactly 262 units of synaptic weight backward and an ordering achieves exactly 262, so irreducible recurrence in the worm's chemical connectome is 3.81%, exactly. The whole brain of a Drosophila larva lies in [11.79%, 13.11%], and the adult Drosophila central brain in [18.96%, 20.87%]. These three intervals do not overlap and increase monotonically with size, across 297 to 21,737 cells and three independent reconstructions. Every ordering was computed from wiring alone, and every one recovers the animal's known input-to-output axis — sensory cells first, muscle or motor neurons last — which is evidence that the orderings are not arbitrary members of a large near-optimal set. We report two methodological traps that invert the result if missed: electrical gap junctions are symmetric and manufacture apparent recurrence, and the synapse-count threshold moves the worm's figure from 3.81% to 6.4%.
