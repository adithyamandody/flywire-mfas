A maximally feedforward ordering of the Drosophila connectome recovers its processing hierarchy and replicates across two brains

Adithya Mandody, independent researcher.

Draft, 25 November 2026. Code and data: https://github.com/adithyamandody/flywire-mfas

Abstract

A connectome is a directed, weighted graph; a brain is a device that turns sensation into action. The simplest bridge between the two is to ask for the ordering of neurons that makes the most synaptic weight point forward — the weighted minimum feedback arc set problem. We compute a high-quality solution for the FlyWire whole-brain connectome of adult Drosophila (136,648 neurons, 41,912,141 synapses), reaching 35,551,569 forward weight (84.824%), above the best figures we are aware of in print, and we prove by cycle packing that no ordering can exceed 90.242%, so the remaining gap is at most 5.4 points. The resulting order, built with no biological input whatsoever, places sensory neurons at the 7.9% mark and motor neurons at 99.8%, and lays the visual pathway out in its correct anatomical sequence. Feedback is not uniform: the outbound visual route is 2.4–4.7% backward while the return routes are 38–75% backward; connections from GABAergic, dopaminergic and serotonergic neurons run backward more often than cholinergic ones; and the two neurons carrying the most backward weight in the whole brain are APL and CT1, the fly's textbook giant inhibitory feedback cells. We then repeat the entire procedure on the independently reconstructed hemibrain connectome of a different fly. The axis, the route asymmetry, the near-absence of feedback onto output neurons, and APL's position at the top all reappear; the glutamate result does not. Across the 2,831 cell types shared by the two connectomes, how much feedback a type carries correlates at Spearman ρ = 0.615 against a shuffled ceiling of 0.057. A single optimisation objective, applied to wiring alone, recovers a hierarchy that two brains agree on.

1. Introduction

Connectomes are now large enough that the problem has shifted from obtaining them to reading them. One natural summary of a directed network is a linear order on its nodes, chosen so that as much edge weight as possible runs forward. For a brain this order is a candidate processing hierarchy: a proposal about what is upstream of what, derived from wiring alone.

Formally, given a weighted directed graph G = (V, E, w) we seek a bijection π: V → {1, …, n} maximising

∑(u,v)∈E, π(u)<π(v) w(u,v)

This is the weighted linear ordering problem, equivalently the weighted minimum feedback arc set problem, and it is NP-hard [11]. The FlyWire Connectome Challenge [12] poses exactly this instance, on the whole-brain reconstruction of the adult female Drosophila brain [1].

Two things make the exercise worth doing carefully. First, a heuristic ordering is only interesting if it is close to the best possible one; otherwise its structure may be an artifact of where the search stopped. Second, a hierarchy extracted from one brain is a hypothesis about brains, and a connectome is one animal. We address both: we certify how much of the gap to optimality remains, and we repeat everything in a second, independently acquired connectome.

Related work. Greedy orderings for feedback arc set go back to Eades, Lin and Smyth [9]. SpringRank [8] produces continuous hierarchy scores for directed networks and we use it as an initialiser. In connectomics, Varshney et al. [10] laid out the C. elegans wiring diagram to reflect signal flow, and Winding et al. [5] analysed feedforward and feedback pathways in the larval Drosophila brain, reporting that 41% of neurons receive long-range recurrent input. On this specific instance, Bader et al.'s Rocket-Crane [6] reports 35,459,266 forward weight (84.60%) and Vahidi [7] reports a forward ratio of 0.8461; both are algorithm papers and neither interprets the resulting order biologically, nor bounds it from above.

Our contribution is (i) a better ordering, (ii) a verified upper bound that says how much room is left, (iii) a biological read-out of the order, and (iv) a replication of that read-out in a second connectome.

2. Results

2.1 An ordering with a certified optimality gap

Our solver reaches 35,551,569 forward weight on the FlyWire challenge graph, 84.824% of the 41,912,141 total, against the challenge's own greedy baseline of 29,023,882 (69.25%). Every number we report was re-scored by an independent implementation written for the purpose.

Because the objective is a maximisation, a heuristic alone cannot say how much is left. We obtain an upper bound by cycle packing. In any ordering, every directed cycle contains at least one backward edge. Assign each directed cycle C a value yC ≥ 0 such that for each edge e, ∑C ∋ e yC ≤ we. Then in any ordering the backward weight is at least ∑C yC, and the forward weight is at most the total minus that sum. A greedy packing on residual capacities — all 2-cycles, then 3-cycles, then longer cycles found by bounded search — yields:

For hemibrain this verified 669,576 cycles (264,098 of length 2, 289,939 of length 3, 115,539 longer) with zero violations.

2.3 Feedforward routes are nearly pure; the feedback is on the return path

15.18% of all synaptic weight (6,360,572) points backward. That weight is very unevenly distributed, and the clearest way to see it is to take each pair of classes and look at both directions between them (Figure 2). There are nine pairs of classes carrying at least 20,000 weight in both directions, and in every one of them one direction is far below the brain-wide rate and the other far above it:

The clean directions span 0.8–8.7%, the return directions 27.3–75.0%, and the brain-wide figure of 15.18% falls strictly between the two in all nine cases. The ordering is not spreading its backward weight around; it is putting almost all of it on one side of each route.

Connections within a class behave differently again. By sheer volume 80% of all backward weight is local — optic→optic 48.1%, central→central 32.0% — but these are the two largest blocks in the connectome and their backward rates, 17.9% and 16.3%, sit close to the brain-wide figure. The structure is in the rates between classes, not in the totals.

Sorting instead by the receiving neuron's class, the most feedback-saturated class in the brain is visual_centrifugal (45.7% of the 485,721 weight it receives), the neurons that by definition carry signals from the central brain back into the optic lobe. At the other end, motor neurons receive 0.9% backward: by the time the brain commits to an action, the loops are behind it.

Cholinergic connections are the most feedforward class of connection in the brain; serotonergic ones the least. Section 2.6 shows that this holds for acetylcholine, GABA and serotonin in a second connectome but not for glutamate.

2.5 The neurons carrying the most feedback are the ones named for it

Ranking individual neurons by the backward weight they carry, the top of the list is APL (two copies, one per hemisphere, 55,913 and 48,211) followed by CT1 (42,467 and 41,217). APL broadly innervates the whole mushroom body [13]; Kenyon cells excite it and it inhibits them in return, and that negative feedback loop is what keeps their odour code sparse [14]. In this graph the loop is direct and nearly complete: 5,176 of the 5,177 Kenyon cells synapse onto an APL and 5,166 receive a synapse back, which the functional experiments on their own could not establish [14]. CT1 is a single giant cell spanning one stratum of the medulla and one of the lobula, with a separate arbour terminal in each column [16] — roughly 700 of them, covering the whole visual field [15]. Both are GABAergic by immunostaining rather than by the predicted labels of §2.4 — APL by anti-GABA [13], CT1 by anti-GAD1 [16] — and both are standard examples of wide-field feedback. The ordering puts them at the top of the feedback ranking without being given any of this.

Below them the list is local interneurons: lobula intrinsic (Li30/32/33), lobula plate intrinsic (LPi12/13), amacrine (Am1), serpentine medulla (Sm42). By feedback per neuron the densest classes are ME.LO (1,130 each) and antennal lobe local neurons (848 each) — the lateral inhibition that implements gain control in the antennal lobe [17].

2.6 Replication in an independent connectome

We repeated the entire pipeline on the hemibrain connectome [3]: a different animal, a different laboratory, a different reconstruction, 21,737 traced non-cropped neurons and 1,764,123 connections of at least 2 synapses. Three independent solver runs landed within 0.02 points of each other and merging them gained nothing, so the search is converged; the result is within 1.91 points of the certified ceiling (§2.1).

Labels are carried from FlyWire by cell type, through the hemibrain_type column of the FlyWire annotations; 15,710 of 21,737 neurons (72%) could be matched. The labels are borrowed; the ordering is not. Cell type is conserved between individuals, while each neuron's position is decided by hemibrain's own wiring.

Every type here except CT1 is in the top 0.11% of both brains. In hemibrain APL carries 105,167 units of backward weight, 2.6× the next neuron and about a thousand times the median. Second to it is DPM, the mushroom body's other wide-field cell: one neuron per hemisphere, projecting over all of the lobes [18], whose output is required during the consolidation of middle-term olfactory memory [19]. CT1 is the explicable exception: it is a single enormous cell spanning the medulla and lobula [15], and the hemibrain volume contains only a cropped fragment of it.

Two further differences follow from what each volume contains rather than from the orderings. Hemibrain's traced set holds just 2 olfactory receptor neurons — the rest are severed at the block boundary — so there is no sensory class to place. And centrifugal neurons move from 16.9% in FlyWire to 91.4% in hemibrain because the optic lobe they feed back into is outside the hemibrain volume; both orderings place them immediately upstream of their targets, and the volumes disagree about where those targets are.

The strongest test uses every cell type at once (Figure 3). For each of the 2,831 cell types present in both connectomes with at least 1,000 synaptic weight, we take the share of that type's weight the ordering must turn around — one number per type per brain — and correlate the two lists:

Agreement is visible at both ends of the range: lLN1_a 44.2% vs 40.9%, APL 43.3% vs 46.4%, PVLP011 40.8% vs 41.0%, down to DNp13 at 0.3% vs 0.3% and DNES2 at 0.0% vs 0.0%.

3. Discussion

A single scalar objective over wiring — point as much synaptic weight forward as possible — reproduces the fly brain's gross functional organisation, and two brains agree on the result at the resolution of individual cell types. That is a stronger statement than "connectomes are hierarchical". It says the hierarchy is recoverable without any functional data, any anatomy, or any prior, and that what it recovers is reproducible across animals.

The most interesting structure is in what refuses to be ordered. Backward weight under an optimal ordering is a lower bound on recurrence: it is the part of the wiring that no hierarchy can explain away. In FlyWire at least 9.76% of all synaptic weight must point backward in any ordering whatsoever (§2.1), and the ordering concentrates the realised backward weight on exactly the pathways and cell types that neuroscience already calls feedback. The method therefore behaves like a detector for recurrence, with a certificate attached.

The hemibrain result sharpens this. Its ordering reaches only 79.13% against FlyWire's 84.82%, but it is within 1.91 points of its own proved ceiling rather than 5.42. The lower figure is not a weaker search: the central brain, with its sensory input severed and no optic lobe to supply easy feedforward weight, simply is more recurrent. Feedforward share is thus a property of a volume that can be measured and compared, provided the bound is computed alongside it.

4. Limitations

  • One ordering of many near-optimal ones, and we have measured what that costs. Six orderings built from different starting layouts, with identical search settings and no merging, land within 0.06% of each other in score while disagreeing about the direction of roughly 1.2 million units of weight — 2.4% of the graph. Across all six, sensory neurons sit at 7.8 ± 0.1% and motor neurons at 99.8 ± 0.1%, so the axis of §2.2 does not depend on which of them is chosen. Finer classes are not all so stable: Kenyon cells and antennal lobe projection neurons move by about twenty points between basins, so the mushroom body's position in the hierarchy is not determined by this method and nothing here rests on it. Single-neuron positions should not be over-read at all.
  • "Backward" means backward in this ordering. Part of the backward weight is unavoidable and we bound it, but which particular edges get turned around depends on the ordering chosen.
  • Labels in the hemibrain analysis are FlyWire's, transferred by cell type. The ordering is independently derived; the labelling is not.
  • Hemibrain is a cropped volume: one hemisphere, no sensory input, no optic lobe. Several differences from FlyWire are attributable to this and are marked where we can tell.
  • Transmitter identities are predictions [4], not measurements, at roughly 94% accuracy per neuron. DPM is a case where the prediction and the immunostaining recorded in the annotations [2] disagree: predicted dopaminergic, measured serotonergic and GABAergic.
  • One position per neuron. Each neuron is a single node here. CT1's terminals have been shown to act as independent functional units [15], so for a cell like it one position summarises hundreds of local circuits rather than describing one.
  • Both connectomes are of female flies, and all results are correlational: they describe wiring, not computation.

5. Methods

Data. FlyWire FAFB v783 connectome and challenge graph [1, 12]; FlyWire annotations, supplemental file 1 of [2]; hemibrain v1.2 traced adjacency export [3], CC-BY 4.0. The hemibrain graph keeps connections of at least 2 synapses between traced, non-cropped neurons, 2 being the smallest weight present in the FlyWire challenge graph, so the two graphs are filtered alike.

Solver. A continuous SpringRank solution [8] provides the initial order. It is then improved by iterated local search over a sifting neighbourhood (remove a neuron, reinsert it at its best position, computed in O(deg log deg) by prefix scan over its neighbours sorted by position). Iterations kick a set of neurons, freeze them while a repair cascade runs over everything that could have changed, then release them; losing iterations are undone exactly from a move log. Four move types are mixed: single-neuron kicks, hub moves (a high-degree neuron plus its strongest wrong-side partners moved together), topological-shuffle kicks following Bader et al. [6] (re-sort a window along a random topological order of the pairs it currently gets right, which cannot lose weight), and group moves (an anchor and its strongest partners lifted out and reinserted as one block at the jointly best position). Parallel searches run on disjoint regions of the order and are combined by an edge-space merge: the child keeps each pair the base gets right unless an island broke it, adds each pair an island fixed, topologically sorts those constraints, and is kept only if it beats the base.

Upper bound. Greedy cycle packing on residual capacities: all 2-cycles, then a sweep over every in/out pair at each node for 3-cycles, then longer cycles by bounded search to depth 6. Every pushed cycle is written out and re-verified independently (§2.1).

Annotation join. Neurons are joined to annotations by root ID (FlyWire) or body ID (hemibrain). For hemibrain, class and transmitter are transferred from FlyWire by majority vote over the FlyWire neurons carrying each hemibrain_type; comma-separated multi-type entries contribute a vote to each component for class transfer, and are excluded entirely from the cell-type correlation of §2.7.

Statistics. Spearman rank correlation with average ranks for ties. The null is 200 random permutations of one side.

Figures. Drawn directly to SVG by mfas_code/figures.py with no plotting library. Figure 2 overlays the hemibrain rate only on routes whose two endpoint classes are both inside the hemibrain volume; routes defined by optic-lobe partners are not comparable between the two datasets and are shown for FlyWire alone.

Code and data. All analysis code is pure-stdlib Python or C with no external dependencies, in the repository above. Both orderings (best_solution.csv, hemibrain_solution.csv) are included, along with the scorer and bound verifier used to check every figure quoted here.

Use of AI assistance. This work was carried out with Claude (Anthropic) throughout. Claude wrote the solver (springrank.c, ilsg.c, tmerge.c), designed and implemented the cycle-packing bound (bound3.c), wrote every analysis script in mfas_code/, ran the experiments, produced the figures and drafted this manuscript; several of the analyses were its own proposals, among them the hemibrain replication (§2.6) and the cell-type correlation (§2.7). The author set the direction and decided at each stage which lines to pursue, and did not rely on Claude's account of its own results: the author ran score.py and check_bound.py, read the solver and the analysis code, read the cited sources, and checked the figures and the numbers quoted here against the underlying data.

Because that is a larger role than a declaration alone can qualify, the central claims are built so as not to rest on trust in the process that produced them. The score of §2.1 is recomputed from the published challenge graph by score.py, a pure-stdlib script that re-reads the graph and the ordering from disk and shares no code with the C solver. The bound is re-verified by check_bound.py, which re-reads the graph and every cycle in the packing, confirms each is a real directed cycle and checks the capacity constraint connection by connection; a bound that could not be re-verified this way was discarded rather than reported, which happened once. Both checks run on public data in minutes. The biological results join published annotations [2,4] to the ordering by identifier, and the principal ones replicate in an independently collected connectome (§2.6).

Fabricated citation is a known failure mode of language models. Every reference here was therefore checked against the PubMed or Crossref record for the work, and the claims resting on each checked against the paper's full text wherever it was reachable.
