A connectome is a directed, weighted graph; a brain is a device that turns sensation into action. The simplest bridge between the two is to ask for the ordering of neurons that makes the most synaptic weight point forward — the weighted minimum feedback arc set problem. We compute a high-quality solution for the FlyWire whole-brain connectome of adult Drosophila (136,648 neurons, 41,912,141 synapses), reaching 35,551,569 forward weight (84.824%), above the best figures we are aware of in print, and we prove by cycle packing that no ordering can exceed 87.374%, so the remaining gap is at most 2.6 points. The resulting order, built with no biological input whatsoever, places sensory neurons at the 7.9% mark and motor neurons at 99.8%, and lays the visual pathway out in its correct anatomical sequence — though we show that most of that axis is also recoverable from a single degree statistic, and scope our claims to what the optimisation adds beyond it. Feedback is not uniform: GABAergic connections run backward twice as often as cholinergic ones, and APL, the mushroom body's giant inhibitory neuron, turns 44% of its own synaptic weight around against a brain-wide 15%. We then repeat the entire procedure on the independently reconstructed hemibrain connectome of a different fly. Compared like for like — central brain against central brain — the axis, the route asymmetry, the near-absence of feedback onto output neurons, APL's rate, and every transmitter result replicate. Across the 2,831 cell types shared by the two connectomes, how much feedback a type carries correlates at Spearman ρ = 0.615, and at 0.607 after controlling for type size. A single optimisation objective, applied to wiring alone, produces an ordering that two independently reconstructed brains agree on at cell-type resolution.
