A connectome is a directed, weighted graph; a brain is a device that turns sensation into action. The simplest bridge between the two is to ask for the ordering of neurons that makes the most synaptic weight point forward — the weighted minimum feedback arc set problem. We compute a high-quality solution for the FlyWire whole-brain connectome of adult Drosophila (136,648 neurons, 41,912,141 synapses), reaching 35,551,569 forward weight (84.824%), above the best figures we are aware of in print, and we prove by cycle packing that no ordering can exceed 90.242%, so the remaining gap is at most 5.4 points. The resulting order, built with no biological input whatsoever, places sensory neurons at the 7.9% mark and motor neurons at 99.8%, and lays the visual pathway out in its correct anatomical sequence. Feedback is not uniform: the outbound visual route is 2.4–4.7% backward while the return routes are 38–75% backward; connections from GABAergic, dopaminergic and serotonergic neurons run backward more often than cholinergic ones; and the two neurons carrying the most backward weight in the whole brain are APL and CT1, the fly's textbook giant inhibitory feedback cells. We then repeat the entire procedure on the independently reconstructed hemibrain connectome of a different fly. The axis, the route asymmetry, the near-absence of feedback onto output neurons, and APL's position at the top all reappear; the glutamate result does not. Across the 2,831 cell types shared by the two connectomes, how much feedback a type carries correlates at Spearman ρ = 0.615 against a shuffled ceiling of 0.057. A single optimisation objective, applied to wiring alone, recovers a hierarchy that two brains agree on.
